Desktop search · research dossier

What to build, and what the measurement says not to

A survey of desktop search on Linux, macOS and Windows; a catalogue of the index structures underneath them; and a design for a new one — checked against a benchmark of the corpus it would actually run on.

Compiled 2026-09-05 Research 11 files, 8,383 lines Tools surveyed 41 Target Rust, permissive, personal workstation

The measured finding

The research began from a premise: a corpus of roughly a million files and 10–200 GB needs an index, because scanning it per query is hopeless. Before writing the recommendation, that premise was measured against the actual corpus in ~/w. It did not survive intact.

Excluding .git, node_modules, target, .venv and their kin leaves 73,565 files and 5.73 GB measured — 12.7% of the files and 3.7% of the bytes. Eighty-seven percent of the file count is machine-generated build output: 260,695 .o files, 8,045 .rlib, 14,864 .bin. One directory, clex, is 74 GB of which 73.5 GB is noise.

On what remains, ripgrep with .gitignore pruning answers a literal query in 0.05 s and a regex with no extractable literal — the exact shape a trigram index exists to accelerate — in 0.05 s as well measured, at over 1000% CPU across 32 threads.

What this changes

The hard problem at this scale is not index density, posting-list compression or query planning. It is exclusion policy: getting the 96% right is worth more than every codec in the survey combined, and a wrong exclusion rule costs 25× the index.

What still justifies building: the document tier, where extraction is expensive and cannot be redone per query; cold-cache behaviour, where the full tree costs 234 s at 0.65 GB/s measured; ranking, which grep has none of; metadata and filename search, which is free; and corpora that are not 96% build output — a mail spool, a document archive, a photo library.

The architecture report opens with this rather than around it, and marks which requirements the measurement demoted.

Method, and what it cannot tell you

Every figure in these reports carries a chip saying how it was sourced. This is the report set's one structural rule, and it exists because the survey turned up more unverifiable numbers than verifiable ones.

Provenance upstream project docs or source paper peer-reviewed benchmark independent vendor vendor-claimed measured measured for this report anecdote community-reported estimate derived arithmetic unknown no figure exists
Three holes in the public record

No independent benchmark exists at this scale. Two research passes looked specifically for a controlled comparison of desktop search tools at ~1M files and 10–200 GB and found none anywhere. That absence is why the measurement in M1 was run at all, and it is an opening: a reproducible harness would be a real contribution.

Every VictoriaLogs density number traces to vendor or vendor-adjacent sources. No fully independent confirmation was found. Since that system is the stated performance reference for this project, its figures should be treated as unverified until measured.

Codec decode speeds are a decade old. The nanoseconds-per-integer figures everyone cites come from 2015 work. The rankings are probably still right; the absolute numbers are not evidence about current hardware.

Two smaller cautions. A 4× discrepancy was found between two third-party sources reporting the same ripgrep benchmark, and is shown rather than averaged away. And the measured corpus is one machine's, heavily weighted toward Rust and JavaScript development — a corpus with a large document archive or mail spool would shift the balance toward extraction, and the measurement should be repeated there before generalising.
