The Desktop Search Landscape

Every desktop search tool worth knowing about on Linux, macOS and Windows, plus the embeddable engines and the log stores that dissent from them — read as intelligence, not as a shortlist. The recurring question is not “is it good” but “what did it choose, and what did that choice cost in bytes and in latency”.

What the survey found

Forty-odd tools were examined across three platforms, plus five embeddable engines and six log/analytics stores that solve an adjacent problem with opposite priorities. Read as a group, they answer a narrower question than “which one is best”: what structure did each one commit to, and what did that commitment cost in bytes and in query latency? The answers cluster far more tightly than the marketing does.

The framing shock

Before any of the survey matters, one measurement on the target corpus changes what the survey is for. On /home/dave/w — 578,200 files, 153.2 GB measured — 87% of the files and 96% of the bytes are machine-generated build output measured. After excluding .git, node_modules, target, .venv and their relatives, what is left is 73,565 files and 5.73 GB measured.1

On that pruned corpus, ripgrep 15.1.0 answers a regex with no extractable literal — [A-Za-z_]+_(index|posting)s?\b, precisely the query shape a trigram index exists to accelerate — in 0.05 s at 1020% CPU measured. A plain literal costs the same 0.05 s. The single strongest argument for a content trigram index evaporates at this corpus size, because no index can be consulted faster than 50 ms of already-parallel scanning. A full metadata crawl of all 578k files costs 1.24 s warm measured; metadata indexing is free.

The seven findings that survive the whole survey

  1. Every long-lived Linux desktop content indexer is Xapian- or Lucene-family, and nothing in the survey uses an LSM tree. Xapian Glass is a copy-on-write B+-tree; Lucene is segment-and-merge; Baloo is LMDB, also a copy-on-write B+-tree.45 The one genuinely different structure in the whole field — livegrep's suffix array — is the one that does not scale.
  2. Daemon resource consumption, not query features, is what kills these tools. It killed Beagle (2006–2009), it has dogged Baloo for a decade, and it is the longest-running complaint about Spotlight's mds. §3.1 works through why Baloo's unusually complete throttling machinery does not fix it.
  3. Content regex is nearly absent across the entire field. Of the Linux full-text apps, only Elasticsearch-backed tools (sist2, Nextcloud FTS) and Obsidian's /pattern/ offer it. Spotlight has none, and neither does anything riding on it. Xapian, Lucene/DocFetcher, Tracker/SPARQL and Baloo all lack it.33 The exception on macOS is FoxTrot, which builds its own index rather than querying Spotlight's — and it is an instructive exception, because it exposes regex only as a secondary criterion, explicitly for performance66. That is not a licensing or effort limitation. An index of terms cannot generate candidates for an arbitrary pattern, so regex can only filter a set that something else narrowed first. Every product here that offers regex over content without an n-gram index has made that same concession somewhere, whether or not the documentation says so.
  4. Several tools that claim regex mean regex over the term dictionary, not over content. Baloo's PostingDB::regexpIter() and Lucene's RegexpQuery (as used by OpenGrok) both enumerate matching terms — grep against a vocabulary list, not grep against bytes.528 This distinction is blurred constantly and is treated as a first-class column in the master table.
  5. Index-size ratios span nearly two orders of magnitude, from 0.04× (Recoll over a PDF corpus, where almost nothing is extractable text) to 3.5× (zoekt, which stores the corpus and positional postings). Most of that spread is a decision about what to store, not about how well it was compressed.
  6. No independent benchmark exists at this scale. R1 and R2 both searched and found nothing: there is no controlled comparison of Recoll vs Tracker vs Baloo vs a Tantivy build at 1M files, and no rigorous fd/fzf-at-1M-files number unknown. Almost every headline figure in this page is the author's own demo. That absence is the reason M1 exists.
  7. The filename problem and the content problem have never been solved in one on-disk format. plocate indexes filenames with trigrams; zoekt and csearch index content with trigrams; nothing in the survey does both coherently in one store.1924
A note on what the numbers are worth

The provenance legend above is not decoration. plocate's headline 466 MB-vs-1.1 GB and 8 ms-vs-20 s figures are the author's own demo with the test corpus unstated;19 zoekt's 3.5× came from a single fetch of a living design document;24 every VictoriaLogs density claim traces to the vendor or to vendor-adjacent writing (§7); and two third-party ripgrep benchmarks disagree by 4× on the same corpus (§3.2). The only figures in this report gathered on the target hardware at the target scale are M1's, and they say the index is less necessary than the field assumes.

Three cells that look comparable and are not

plocate's 0.42× is index-vs-index. It is 466 MB against mlocate's 1.1 GB for the same tree, not against corpus bytes — and plocate is storing strictly more information (a real inverted index versus a flat sorted list) in 42% of the space. Do not read it as a density figure against content.19

Everything's ~45 MB / 1M files is per-file, not per-byte. Corpus size in GB is irrelevant to it: it indexes only the namespace. At the 5M-file end of the target that is ~500 MB resident for filenames alone, before any content index. Its efficiency story is “small enough to hold entirely in RAM”, not “compact on disk”.

Recoll's 0.04× is a corpus artefact, not a codec win. The test corpus is 18,000 PDFs totalling 30 GB — overwhelmingly page images and font tables, with very little extractable text per megabyte. Recoll's own docs warn the ratio inverts on mbox archives, where the index can end up larger than the source.2

Linux

Linux is the only platform in the survey where the full-text lineage and the filename/code lineage are entirely separate populations of tools with no overlap. Nothing indexes both well. The full-text side has converged on two engine families and one recurring failure mode; the filename/code side has converged on one algorithm and one dominant implementation per problem.

Full-text desktop search

Recoll / Xapian is the density winner in the whole survey, and the caveat matters more than the number. Xapian's Glass backend is a copy-on-write B+-tree with a postlist table and a termlist table — the termlist exists so deleting or reindexing one document can find every posting list it must touch without a scan.4 Recoll 1.24/1.25 changed the format again to speed phrase search by storing document text inside the index, a deliberate growth-for-speed trade upstream.3 The term dictionary is a sorted B-tree, not an FST, which is precisely why the query language offers prefix wildcards and no general regex: a B-tree gives cheap prefix range scans and no efficient automaton intersection.

Its architectural distinction is negative and underrated: Recoll does not require a daemon. Indexing is a batch or cron job, with inotify monitoring optional (recollindex -m, subject to fs.inotify.max_user_watches). Every recommendation of Recoll as “the one that doesn't hurt” may be about that fact rather than about its throttling code — R1 flags that it did not cleanly separate the two.

Tracker / TinySPARQL and LocalSearch is architecturally the outlier on this list: an RDF triple store implemented on SQLite, where filesystem metadata, EXIF, ID3 tags and extracted document text are all triples, and full-text is the fts:match predicate — one predicate among many rather than the primary interface. The 2024 GNOME rename was a rebrand, not a rewrite: the binary and package names changed, the engine and file formats did not.9

Baloo's LMDB schema — the one genuinely different structure here

Baloo does not use segments and does not merge. Every database class under src/engine/ includes lmdb.h and operates on MDB_dbi/MDB_txn handles: a single memory-mapped, copy-on-write B+-tree environment holding about ten named sub-databases, updated transactionally in place per write batch.56

Two consequences follow directly from that schema and are worth stating as design intelligence rather than criticism. First, LMDB buys ACID crash-consistency and gives up the append-then-merge write pattern that lets Lucene and Tantivy batch a large indexing run cheaply — a real tradeoff, not a mistake. Second, building document IDs out of st_dev and st_ino ties the index to the local filesystem's device and inode numbering, which is a hard constraint for bind mounts, network filesystems and filesystem migration. KDE's own wiki calls Baloo “decentralized, no central database”; that is a claim about its three cooperating processes, and the storage layer is considerably more conventional than the framing suggests.

Baloo's PostingDB::regexpIter(const QRegularExpression &, const QByteArray &prefix) walks the term dictionary under a prefix and validates each term against the pattern. That is real regex — over indexed terms, equivalent to grep against a vocabulary list. It cannot match inside a word the tokenizer split differently and cannot cross term boundaries.5

Key finding — throttling is not the answer

All three Linux indexers converge on the identical baseline: nice(19) + ioprio idle class + SCHED_IDLE, read from primary source in each case.71012 This triad is table stakes, not a differentiator. Baloo goes furthest — it is the only one that also ships default cgroup limits (Slice=background.slice, CPUWeight=1, IOWeight=1, MemoryHigh=25%) and the only one with a confirmed unconditional stop-content-indexing-on-battery gate — and it has the worst and longest-running reputation for exactly the failure mode all of that machinery targets, with reports as recent as August 2024.8

The mechanical reason is that none of the triad is a ceiling: nice, ioprio, SCHED_IDLE and cgroup CPUWeight are all relative-to-contention. On an otherwise idle desktop a SCHED_IDLE extractor still gets 100% of a free core for free. And MemoryHigh throttles allocation rather than capping it, while the complaints are frequently about RSS. Recoll's own FAQ says so outright and tells administrators to layer systemd-run --property=CPUQuota= on top.11

R1 could not distinguish two remaining explanations, and neither can this page: (a) total work volume — a million files of content extraction is simply that many CPU-seconds regardless of scheduling class; or (b) extractor correctness — one file that hangs inside poppler or exiv2 burns its low-priority slice indefinitely. That is the most important open question the Linux slice surfaced and did not answer.

Recoll holds one mechanism nobody else in the survey uses: if choom is present it shells out to choom -n 300 -p <pid>, making the indexer a preferred OOM-killer victim.12 That targets a different failure mode entirely — dying cleanly under memory pressure rather than taking something else down with you — and for a design that prefers a dense index (meaning more RAM pressure at merge, not less) it belongs in the requirements, not bolted on after complaints the way Recoll's own issue history suggests it was.

Nothing in any of the three implements queue-on-user-activity. None of the source trees contain input-idle detection — X11/Wayland idle time, foreground-CPU watching — that pauses indexing on interactive activity as opposed to battery state or raw priority. “Don't index while the user is compiling” has no prior art here.

The Beagle post-mortem

Beagle's last release was 0.3.9 in January 2009.15 Four causes, in ascending order of transferability:

  1. Mono was a distribution tax, not just a runtime cost. Distros without a mature Mono stack simply did not ship it, capping the addressable install base independent of quality.
  2. Resource use was never solved, and the team knew they were blind. The GUADEC 2006 material admits “the tools to profile and debug them were largely non-existent” — flying blind on precisely the class of problem that later sank Baloo's reputation.
  3. Scope creep inherited from Dashboard: chat logs, IM, web history, contacts, all beyond “index my files”.
  4. Loss of institutional backing. GNOME chose Tracker. Once the desktop environment does not depend on your indexer, nothing forces it to keep going.

The load-bearing pattern: every daemon-based content indexer on Linux has hit the same background-extractor-eats-a-core failure mode. The survivors survived by being installed by default with a desktop environment, not by solving it. Baloo still has not.

Two other entries deserve a line each. sist2 is a crawler and extractor that writes a portable archive and delegates search to a separate Elasticsearch instance — so it gets regex, wildcard and fuzzy queries for free and pays Elasticsearch's JVM heap and index size for them, which is the real cost of that stack at desktop scale. Paperless-ngx migrated Whoosh to Tantivy through 2025 explicitly for Whoosh's performance ceiling, and had to build a regex-based query-rewrite shim to keep old syntax working — a shim that PR #13010 shows reaching its limits.17 That is a directly relevant data point on what a clean backend swap under a stable query surface actually costs.

The ecosystem's own vote

Ulauncher's content-search extensions wrap Recoll, Tracker, DocFetcher or locate. Nobody wraps Baloo, despite it shipping on every KDE desktop. Read that as an implicit reputation and API-friction signal sitting on top of the explicit CPU/RAM complaints.

The 2024–2026 newcomers are all Rust and all Tantivy: Universal-Local-AI-Indexer (Tantivy + SQLite, Windows), ultrasearch (NTFS MFT for names, Tantivy for content, split-process), ygrep (Tantivy, framed as code search for AI coding assistants). No credible new Go-based Linux content indexer was found at all. Tantivy is the emerging default for new entrants; none of them is yet a production-maturity general desktop indexer.

Filename and code search

plocate is the densest design in the survey and its density comes from one deliberate coarsening. Filenames are concatenated into compressed blocks before the trigram index is built, so posting lists point at blocks, not filenames. The upstream documentation states the tradeoff outright: bigger compression context and fewer posting-list elements, at the price of less precise postings and more false positives to weed out after intersection.19 Queries trigram the pattern, intersect fewest-postings-first (the same ordering csearch and zoekt use), decompress the surviving blocks and substring-check. I/O is issued through io_uring where available — which matters far more on spinning disks than on NVMe.

This is exactly the trade a density-preferring design wants, applied to filenames: spend a little latency, save a lot of bytes. Its sibling is ugrep-indexer, which is not a trigram index at all — it is a per-file Bloom filter over content-derived hashes, using N² hash functions instead of the textbook N because the author found standard parameterisation has too high a false-positive rate for the short substrings typical of code search, plus a cheaper zero-false-positive pre-check that rejects a file if any byte of the pattern's first 8 bytes never occurs in it.29 Fixed cost per file regardless of file length; all imprecision pushed to query time. These two are the designs most worth studying for a dense build, and they sit at the opposite end of the spectrum from zoekt and livegrep.

The scaling law is the real distinction, not the ratio. Trigram index size scales with the corpus's trigram vocabulary; a suffix array scales with corpus bytes — one 4- or 8-byte integer per byte of source. That is why livegrep was built for one large but bounded corpus (Linux kernel HEAD) and why it does not survive contact with 200 GB without compressed-suffix-array or FM-index machinery the reference implementation does not have.23

zoekt's other constraints are worth recording: shards are memory-mapped single files capped near 4 GB because offsets are uint32 (~1 GB of source content each once overhead is counted); case-insensitive queries expand each literal into its case-variant OR-set rather than folding case in the index, which is the same selectivity loss Cox describes; and updates are whole-repo reindex per shard on HEAD movement, not file-level incremental — a clean fit for Sourcegraph's git-anchored model and a structural mismatch for a live desktop filesystem where files change outside any commit boundary.24

Cox's numbers remain the reference: on the DATAKIT query the trigram index cut candidates from 2,739 files to 3 before the verify pass; case-sensitive searches ran roughly 100× faster than brute force and case-insensitive roughly 10–15×, because OR-ing case variants is strictly less selective than a single AND chain.21

A 4× contradiction in the unindexed baseline

The number an index has to beat is disputed by a factor of four, in third-party sources, on what is described as the same corpus.

ripgrep.dev's own benchmark page (i9-12900K, 64 GB, NVMe, Ubuntu 22.04, hyperfine, median of 10, warm cache) reports 6.73 s for “Sherlock Holmes” over a 13.5 GB concatenated Project Gutenberg file — about 2.0 GB/s benchmark.27 A separately-surfaced DEV Community article claims 1.664 s for what it describes as the same scenario benchmark. R2 could not reconcile them and neither can this page. Do not average them and do not silently prefer one. A 4× uncertainty on the exact baseline an index is supposed to beat is not a rounding error.

M1 sidesteps the dispute by measuring the corpus that actually matters: rg -uuu over the full 153.2 GB ran at 0.65–0.71 GB/s at 99–106% CPU — I/O-bound on one core's worth of work, reading bytes nobody wants indexed measured.1

The rest of the same table: on the Linux 6.6 source tree (~75k files, ~900 MB) matching [A-Z]+_SUSPEND, ripgrep took 0.082 s against ugrep 0.301 s (3.7×), git grep 0.273 s (3.3×), GNU grep 0.671 s (8.2×), ag 0.443 s (5.4×) and ack 3.231 s (39.4×) benchmark.27 Every actively-maintained tool here that supports real regex uses RE2 or an RE2-equivalent linear-time engine — csearch, zoekt, livegrep — specifically to avoid backtracking blowup in the verify pass. The Rust regex crate is the same guarantee, so that choice is validated prior art rather than a convenience default.

Two holes in the record, and one absence that is a finding

plocate publishes no build time or build throughput — not in the man pages, not on the project page unknown. Build time on a 1M-file corpus is a first-order design constraint and the number does not exist publicly.

No rigorous fd/fzf-at-1M-files benchmark exists unknown. The unindexed walk-time tools publish nothing because their pitch is that a warm-cache walk on NVMe is fast enough not to need an index — which is exactly the claim an index exists to falsify. M1 measured it directly: find over 578k files, warm, is 1.24 s measured. The claim holds.

No new plocate- or zoekt-class Rust project exists. The trigram-index-for-code-search space has had one dominant living implementation (zoekt) for years and the filename-index space has one (plocate). Either the problem is hard to get right, or it is an opening.

macOS

macOS is the platform where the most is known about the architecture and the least about the format. Spotlight is a pipeline, not a daemon: mds owns the store and schedules work, mdworker processes do per-file extraction in parallel and sandboxed (so a hung importer on one file type does not block the pipeline), and mdimporter bundles — under /System/Library/Spotlight/, /Library/Spotlight/, or inside any app bundle at Contents/Library/Spotlight/ — parse one or more UTIs and return a dictionary of kMDItem* attributes plus extracted text.31

Metadata and content are one attribute space. An importer returns a single dictionary mixing filesystem attributes (owner, size, kind) with content-derived fields (document text, EXIF, ID3), and Spotlight indexes all of it through the same pipeline. That is why an mdfind/NSMetadataQuery predicate can mix kMDItemKind == 'PDF' with kMDItemTextContent == '*foo*' in one expression. It is the single most copyable idea in the platform, and its inverse — building a metadata database and a full-text index that cannot be joined in one query — is the mistake it avoids.

FSEvents: the durable change log

Spotlight does not poll. fseventsd consumes the kernel's /dev/fsevents and persists a rolling per-volume log — /System/Volumes/Data/.fseventsd/ since Big Sur, /.fseventsd before it — in a proprietary gzip-compressed binary format Apple has never published.34 Two properties separate it from anything Linux offers:

  • Directory granularity, not file granularity. An event says “something changed under this directory” with flags and a monotonically increasing global event ID; the consumer must stat/readdir to find out what. inotify and fanotify report the specific path inline. FSEvents trades precision for coalescing — a burst of writes to one file collapses to one notification.
  • It is durable and replayable. A consumer that was not running can resume from its last known event ID. inotify cannot do this at all: it has no persistent log, and a watcher that is not running misses events entirely. fanotify has no built-in durable log either.

A Linux indexer that wants “catch up from where I left off, even after a crash, without a full rescan” has to build something FSEvents-shaped itself. That is real work the platform will not do for you.

What is genuinely not public about the store

Apple has never published Spotlight's on-disk format. Everything known comes from digital-forensics reverse engineering, principally Yogesh Khatri's spotlight_parser, with derivative tooling in mac_apt and plaso.32 What that establishes: locations (/.Spotlight-V100/Store-V2/<UUID>/ per volume; ~/Library/Metadata/CoreSpotlight/index.spotlightV3/ per user since High Sierra, when Apple consolidated Safari history, Notes, Mail metadata and Maps into it), record framing, category and property enumeration, per-value type tags, and forensically interesting attributes — last-opened timestamps and open counts that exist in no other artefact.

What it does not establish, and what this page will therefore not claim: the posting-list encoding, the term dictionary structure, or any compression scheme unknown. The live.N.M files in the store directory look like write-ahead journals and their commit semantics are inferred from filenames and DFIR chatter, not from a spec unknown. Khatri's blog promised a follow-up format post that R3 could not find. The closest thing to a public specification is spotlight_parser.py itself.

Sources routinely call the store “an inverted index” as settled fact; the only primary-ish evidence documents records, categories and properties without ever confirming that term. Treat it as the consensus description, not a structural fact.

The query grammar, and the industry it created

Apple's own predicate-comparison reference is the best primary source on what the language cannot do.33 Comparisons must be KEY operator VALUE and never reversed; substring match is written myAttribute == '*foo*' (glob wildcards, not LIKE or regex); case and diacritic sensitivity are trailing modifier letters on the literal ('foo'cd). And then: no regex, and no phrase or proximity operator, twenty-plus years in upstream. Multi-word literals are matched as a substring pattern, not as an adjacent-phrase query.

That gap is the entire business model of the third-party market:

  • HoudahSpot maintains no index at all. It is a query builder, saved-search templates, and hundreds of result columns drawn from the kMDItem* space that the stock UI never exposes. It inherits every Spotlight limit verbatim — a paid product that is proof a well-specified, attribute-rich query capability is valuable independent of any UI, and equally proof a good front-end cannot fix a thin engine.
  • Alfred queries Spotlight directly. Raycast is the hybrid: its own local filename/metadata index (structure unpublished unknown), but its own manual states content search falls back to the OS index and is disabled wherever Spotlight is not covering a folder.
  • DEVONthink is the most architecturally distinct: it does not search the live filesystem in normal use, it imports documents into its own store. Its differentiators are IR concepts surfaced in the UI — Concordance (a browsable word-frequency view over a document or the whole database), Classify (find the statistical pattern distinguishing a group from its siblings, then place new documents), and See Also (similarity retrieval over the whole database rather than a typed query). The algorithm has never been published unknown — TF-IDF vector similarity, Bayesian, something else, unknown. Community threads exist precisely because power users are reverse-engineering it from the outside.36
  • Foxtrot Professional Search is the existence proof that matters: an independent full-text index built by a small team, with Boolean, wildcards, term exclusion, a drag-adjustable word-distance proximity slider, and real regex, covering local disks, externals and network volumes Spotlight covers badly, and partitioned per project rather than per volume.35 Its index format is not published unknown. It settles the question of whether Spotlight's grammar is a platform ceiling: it is not, it is a decision.
  • Find Any File and EasyFind take the opposite bet entirely: no index, scan on every query. Find Any File's selling point is a guarantee of completeness — it cannot miss a file to stale index state, corruption, or an excluded location. EasyFind adds regex on names, live content search, and looks inside .app bundles Spotlight treats as opaque. Their sustained popularity is direct evidence that a meaningful fraction of Mac power users do not trust the built-in index to be complete.

What goes wrong, and what it argues for

Spotlight's failure list rhymes exactly with Baloo's and Beagle's. mds/mds_stores/mdworker runaway CPU is a decades-long recurring complaint, usually triggered by a bad importer, a large untracked change set, or index corruption. There is no proactive corruption detection and no “your index is stale” signal: users notice symptoms, then run mdutil -i off, delete .Spotlight-V100, mdutil -i on, mdutil -E — an all-or-nothing rebuild with no incremental-repair path exposed. Sonoma-era regressions sustained threads on Apple's own developer forums, and Eclectic Light was still publishing systematic “quirks of Spotlight” investigations in September 2025.31

The importer plugin model has the security cost that goes with ambient trust: mdimporter bundles auto-load from app bundles and run against attacker-controlled bytes, which has made them a documented macOS persistence technique and, in 2025, the vector for the “Sploitlight” TCC bypass. A stable public extractor plugin API is the right idea and ambient trust for its plugins is the wrong implementation — treat “parses attacker-controlled bytes” as the threat model for every format plugin from day one.

Three further constraints transfer as negatives worth designing against. Index scope is coupled to volume boundaries — indexing is essentially per-volume on/off, which is precisely why Foxtrot's per-project partitioning is a feature users pay for; design indices as user-scoped collections of paths, not as a property of which disk a file is on. Network volume coverage is inconsistent and often disabled by default, which is the gap the direct-scan tools fill. And index health is hidden — expose freshness as a first-class checkable state and support incremental repair rather than nuke-and-rebuild.

Windows

Windows holds both the best engineering in the survey and its worst cautionary tale, and they are the same problem solved at opposite ends.

Everything: MFT plus USN

Everything's entire speed advantage is a refusal to use FindFirstFile/FindNextFile at all.38

  1. Initial build: open the raw volume (\\.\C:) and read the Master File Table in one sequential pass. The MFT is the record NTFS already maintains — one entry per file or directory, with name, parent reference, size, timestamps — so first-index time is proportional to MFT size rather than to the number of FindNextFile round-trips a crawl needs.
  2. Ongoing: stop reading the MFT and poll the USN change journal — NTFS's append-only log of every create, delete, rename and attribute change — roughly once a second, applying deltas to the in-memory index. The journal is a capped circular log; if the service has been off long enough that it has wrapped past the last read position, the fallback is a fresh MFT re-read for that volume (inferred from how USN journals behave, not documented by voidtools unknown).
  3. Privilege: both raw volume handles and USN reads need elevation. voidtools' answer is the Everything Service — installed once, it holds the elevated handle persistently, and the ordinary non-admin client talks to the service. Privilege separation as a product feature, and directly transferable.
  4. Non-NTFS volumes — FAT, exFAT, network shares, optical — have no MFT and no journal, so Everything falls back to conventional folder indexing: an ordinary recursive scan that cannot track changes made while it was not watching. voidtools' own docs separate “NTFS index”, “ReFS index”, “Folder index” and “File List” as distinct types upstream. ReFS is first-class at the “it works” level; the mechanism is not published.
The honest verdict on matching it

The mechanism cannot be matched on Linux; the outcome at the target scale probably can. There is no cross-filesystem MFT. ext4, XFS, Btrfs and ZFS each keep inode metadata in incompatible on-disk formats and none exposes a supported bulk “give me every inode’s metadata in one read” interface. Reading them directly means one bespoke, format-version-sensitive reader per filesystem instead of one MFT reader that covers essentially every Windows disk — which is almost certainly why nothing plays this trick on Linux. Btrfs's subvolume find-new and generation numbers are the nearest analogue and are neither general nor always-on.

For the change-tracking half, fanotify with FAN_MARK_FILESYSTEM watches a whole mounted filesystem without one watch descriptor per directory (inotify's classic scaling wall). It differs from USN in two ways that matter: it is a live stream with no persistent backing log — if the daemon is not running, or an event is dropped under buffer pressure, that change is gone — and the filesystem-wide mark needs CAP_SYS_ADMIN.

And the scale argument settles it. At ~1M files even a walk that is one to two orders of magnitude slower per entry than an MFT read is seconds of wall-clock, not minutes; the two designs only diverge sharply at NTFS's home scale of tens of millions of files. M1 measured the Linux side directly: 578,200 files enumerated in 1.24 s warm measured.1 Everything's cold-start is not the bar; the actual corpus size is.

Two things about Everything are not known and should not be asserted. Its in-memory name structure is closed-source, no credible reverse-engineering writeup exists, and any claim of a trie, suffix array or sorted string table is speculation unknown. And which regex engine backs its regex: function is unpublished unknown — the syntax surface discussed on its forums (named groups, lookaheads, (?m), a multiline: modifier) is consistent with a PCRE-family engine, which is an inference, not a fact.39

What is worth reverse-specifying is the query micro-language: size: with ranges and a metric: variant, dm: and sibling date functions (dm:today, dm:april), content: combinable with regex:, ext: type: folder: file: empty: dupe: attrib: parent: child: sizerank:, booleans with parenthesised grouping, and glob wildcards distinct from and combinable with regex — all evaluated live as the user types.64 None of those primitives require anything NTFS-specific.

The anti-pattern inside the best design

Everything's index efficiency story is “small enough to hold entirely in RAM”, not “compact on disk”: no compression, no B-tree overhead, because the structure is one process's heap. ~100 MB RAM per 1M files anecdote (independent threads span 75–200 MB, higher during active indexing, and no vendor number exists unknown), with ~45 MB per million persisted anecdote. At 5M files that is ~500 MB resident for filenames alone. It never had to solve the on-disk density problem because it never put the index on disk in a compact form. Study the update mechanism; do not copy the index model.

Windows Search and Windows.edb

Windows Search decomposes into SearchIndexer.exe (owns the ESE database and the queue, exposes the OLE DB provider, ISearchQueryHelper and the search-ms: protocol), SearchProtocolHost.exe (hosts protocol handlers that enumerate a namespace), and SearchFilterHost.exe — which runs IFilters and property handlers as a separate, low-integrity, sandboxed process specifically because parsing arbitrary untrusted file formats is the highest-risk code in the pipeline.41 That isolation decision is the one unambiguously right call in the design and maps directly onto Linux, where poppler and LibreOffice's filters are exactly the code you do not want inside a privileged daemon.

An IFilter emits chunks of extracted text with position information (so phrase and proximity queries are possible) plus chunks of property values; the indexer word-breaks and normalizes before insertion. Property handlers cover structured metadata independently. The gathering tables (SystemIndex_Gthr, SystemIndex_GthrPth) hold the work queue, which is how a bulk copy or a resumed-after-sleep indexer catches up incrementally.

On disk it is a single ESE (JET Blue) file — a B-tree ISAM engine, the same one behind NTDS.dit and historically Exchange — at C:\ProgramData\Microsoft\Search\Data\Applications\Windows\Windows.edb. Microsoft does not document the internal table layout as a stable contract, so the best available source is forensic literature parsing the file directly. What that literature describes is structurally interesting: rather than a separate inverted-index table distinct from a documents table, SystemIndex_PropertyStore carries both structured properties and extracted content — including System_Search_AutoSummary, a text snippet of the file — with the word-level index encoded inside ESE's own structures rather than any obviously named table.42

And that is the whole size problem. Microsoft's own KB is titled “The Windows.edb file grows very large” and describes cases exceeding 50 GB upstream; community reports include 107 GiB on a 278 GiB drive and 92 GB on a 500 GB drive anecdote, roughly 20–35% of source volume.40 Microsoft publishes no expected ratio unknown; the guidance is “exclude the offending locations and rebuild”. The cited root causes are unbounded per-item content snippets and whole-file reindexing of continuously appended logs, so the property store accumulates re-extracted text over a file's lifetime rather than a bounded per-file cost. This is accidental bloat, not a chosen tradeoff — it is larger than dtSearch's worst published ratio for a system with no reputation for being fast either.

AQS supports property-scoped queries, uppercase-only boolean operators (the one case-sensitive part of an otherwise case-insensitive grammar), quoted exact phrases, and stem-based prefix behaviour rather than true globs. No native regex upstream, and no proximity operator found in first-party documentation — R4 records that as “not found” rather than “does not exist” unknown.43 Its fourth failure mode is worth naming because it is a design choice, not a bug: a file outside an indexed location, or one whose IFilter is not registered, simply does not appear in results, with no slow-scan fallback of the kind Everything or grep would give.

dtSearch and the rest

dtSearch is the only vendor in the entire survey publishing a defensible index-size ratio: “generally about 1/8 to 1/3 the size of the original documents”, trending toward ~15% on large collections vendor.44 It is a licensable engine, not just an app — it sits inside legal and eDiscovery platforms such as Relativity — with native regex behind a ## prefix, combinable with fuzzy, stemming and w/n proximity in the same query.45 No independent latency numbers were found unknown, but 15% at multi-GB-to-TB scale, in a market that would notice if queries were slow, is the single best external figure to check a density claim against.

FileLocator Pro / Agent Ransack contributes one structural idea worth more than its market share: two explicit modes, Lite scanning live with zero index, Pro maintaining one with real-time update — and regex offered independently on the filename box and the content box in the same query, which almost nothing else does.46 X1, Copernic and Lookeen differentiate on breadth of connected sources (Outlook, Exchange, PST federation) rather than query power and publish no index figures unknown.

Engines and formats

Every long-lived tool above is a client of one of five formats. This section is the ledger of what each format chose and what the choice cost, ranked on bytes rather than QPS. The consistent finding across all of them is stated first because it determines how the whole table reads.

The distinction the field blurs

Regex over the term dictionary walks the vocabulary — the sorted or FST-encoded list of distinct terms — with a compiled automaton and turns matching terms into an OR of exact-term postings lookups. It is fast, proportional to vocabulary size rather than corpus size, and it is what Tantivy's RegexQuery, Lucene's RegexpQuery, Bleve's, Elasticsearch's regexp, and Baloo's regexpIter all do. It only ever matches whole tokens as the tokenizer produced them: no cross-token spans, no matching inside a word the analyzer split differently, nothing the analyzer stripped.

Regex over document content inspects the original byte stream, as grep does. None of the classic inverted-index engines do this natively. The one format-level answer in the entire survey is the trigram-shortlist pattern — index every overlapping 3-gram, intersect trigram postings to shortlist candidate documents, then run a real regex engine over the survivors. SQLite FTS5's trigram tokenizer is the only mainstream engine that ships the shortlist half; csearch, zoekt and livegrep are the tools that built the whole thing.

A tool advertising “regex search” that means only the first kind will silently fail on patterns spanning tokenizer boundaries, case folding, or stemmed forms. True content regex is not a term-dictionary feature at all — it is a separate n-gram shortlist stage in front of a real regex engine, and every mainstream engine treats it as an afterthought or omits it.

The arithmetic: bloom filter versus posting list

VictoriaLogs sizes its bloom filters at ~2 bytes per unique token per block-column upstream — 1,000 unique words costs ~2 KB, 20,000 costs ~40 KB. That is deliberately not tuned to a target false-positive rate; it is a flat, predictable budget, accepting a higher FPR than a textbook Bloom filter at that bit budget in exchange for simplicity.52 For comparison, the standard results: a classical Bloom filter needs ~9.6 bits/element at 1% FPR and ~14.4 at 0.1%; Xor8 is ~10 bits/key at 0.39% FPR upstream; Binary Fuse8 is ~9 bits/key at 0.39% and Binary Fuse16 ~18 bits/key at 0.0015% paper — and binary fuse filters remain zstd-compressible after construction, which classical Bloom filters do not.62

The structural difference is the whole argument, and it is not about compression at all. A bloom-style block filter is a membership-per-block structure costing O(blocks) × a constant, essentially independent of how often the term occurs inside a block. A term appearing once in a block costs the same bits as a term appearing a thousand times. A posting list costs O(occurrences) × bytes-per-posting, strictly linear. So:

  • Low-frequency terms scattered across many blocks: the two converge, but the bloom filter never writes exact positions and wins on bytes.
  • High-frequency terms concentrated in few blocks: the posting list for that block is one entry plus in-block positions, while the bloom filter pays its flat per-block budget regardless. Bloom filters are indifferent to intra-block frequency — which is exactly why they are cheap, and exactly why once inside a flagged block you get zero help finding the matching rows.
  • At VictoriaLogs' own numbers: a 2 MiB block with 20,000 unique tokens costs 40 KB of bloom regardless of total occurrences, which could easily be 200,000+. A positional inverted index over that same block exceeds 40 KB the moment average term frequency passes about 1 — i.e. always. The order-of-magnitude saving comes from throwing away all intra-block addressing, not just positions.

Execution follows from the structure. LogsQL prunes by tenant and time partition (free — directory layout), prunes blocks by stream and min/max timestamp from the in-RAM metaindex.bin and index.bin (headers only, no decompression), tests each surviving block-column's bloom filter, and decompresses and linearly scans only the survivors. It is filter-pushdown-with-prefilter, the same family as ClickHouse skip indexes and Parquet row-group pruning. The consequence: a rare term over a wide unfiltered range still touches every non-skipped block's filter — O(blocks in range) — where a real inverted index jumps straight to the posting list in O(log terms). VictoriaLogs wins when blocks are prunable and loses on needle-in-haystack with no time or stream narrowing.

Every VictoriaLogs number traces to the vendor

“usually compresses logs by 10x or more” vendor; “15–30x for typical log data, up to 100x” vendor, restated by a third party without independent measurement; “up to 30x less RAM, up to 15x less disk space” than Elasticsearch vendor; “up to 1000x faster than Grafana Loki” vendor. The one third-party benchmark R10 found — 3× ingestion, 72% less CPU, 87% less memory, 12× faster search — is from a partner consultancy benchmark with methodology and independence unverified.55

R10 found no benchmark run by a party with no commercial stake, with disclosed raw data and a repeatable methodology unknown. Worse, the “at least 8 bytes per token” figure attributed to Elasticsearch-style inverted indexes — the denominator in VictoriaMetrics' own 4×-smaller claim, and therefore the load-bearing number in the whole thesis — is vendor-stated without a cited derivation.54

The 1000x-vs-Loki figure is mechanically plausible, which is not the same as verified: Loki genuinely does zero indexing of log bodies, so any query not satisfied by label selection is a full decompress-and-grep. Read it as an upper bound for a rare term over a low-selectivity range, not a typical case.

The contradiction: ClickHouse went the other way

VictoriaMetrics' materials frame “not an inverted index” as categorically superior. ClickHouse — a comparable columnar system that shipped exactly the bloom-filter-over-granules design in tokenbf_v1 and ngrambf_v1 — found that approach wanting, re-implemented a real native inverted text index from scratch starting February 2025, reached beta in December 2025 and General Availability around March 2026, reporting 10–50× better query latency than the bloom-filter approach and 7–10× faster cold queries vendor.58

Two things about it are worth noting precisely. It is a real token-based inverted index, not a bloom filter. And it is explicitly not a relevance engine — no BM25, no TF-IDF, purpose-built for filter-and-aggregate over billions of rows rather than ranked retrieval. So the direction of travel is not “columnar plus bloom is always sufficient” and it is not a return to Lucene either: it is “just enough inverted index, no ranking”. The observability world is bifurcating, not converging on VictoriaLogs' end of the spectrum, and it is bifurcating for exactly the query patterns bloom filters serve worst.

What transfers, and what does not

The log lineage optimises for a data shape a desktop corpus does not have: append-mostly, recency-skewed, discarded after a retention window, ingested continuously at high volume, and queried rarely per byte stored. Daily partitions, hot/cold tiering, “do not index what will probably never be queried”, accepting 100ms-plus because operators run ad hoc investigations — all of it follows from that shape. A desktop corpus is the mirror image: largely static, read-mostly, queried interactively and repeatedly against the same working set, and two to four orders of magnitude smaller than what these systems target.

Three consequences, stated bluntly rather than in deference to a design worth admiring:

  1. The central saving has no analog. The log lineage skips per-occurrence structure because most of the data will age out unqueried. Files do not age out, and “search all my files” is the common case and it recurs. That removes the strongest argument for bloom-over-blocks.
  2. The central cost is a worse fit here than there. Block-granularity results with no intra-block addressing are fine for logs, where a line is already the natural unit of a hit. A 50,000-line source file is not: you want line and offset results, which pushes back toward wanting some per-occurrence structure even if it is only a block-relative offset table — much smaller than full positions, but not free.
  3. Regex is precisely what none of them accelerate. VictoriaLogs' re(), Loki's |~, ClickHouse's match() all fall through to linear scan once past coarse pruning — a regex has no fixed token set to hash, so the bloom filter cannot help by construction. This is documented behaviour, not a hidden gap, and it is the sharpest edge of the design for a regex-oriented workload.

What does transfer, cleanly and cheaply: columnar per-field storage with dictionary encoding then zstd for metadata (path, extension, mtime, size, mime type) — pure win, no argument for anything heavier; zone maps and time partitioning, where the partition boundary is the zone map and the pruning is done by directory naming before any index opens — free for mtime-range and path-prefix queries, worthless for content; and Quickwit's hotcache idea, which needs no object storage to be useful: a tiny always-resident “which byte ranges do I need” summary in front of a heavy on-disk structure is cheap regardless of whether the heavy structure is dense or sparse. Pagefind's partition-by-first-letters-and-lazy-fetch is the same instinct from the static-site world.

The layering pattern is Splunk's, not VictoriaLogs'

The technique R10 concluded is most worth building in is not bloom-filter-as-primary-index. It is Splunk's layering: a cheap, flat-cost bloom filter at coarse file or block granularity purely to skip whole files before touching anything more expensive, sitting in front of a real — but non-positional or sparsely-positional — per-file structure that does the actual matching. Bloom as a prefilter, not a substitute. Combine it with VictoriaLogs' columnar-dictionary-then-zstd encoding for the metadata columns and you get most of the density win and none of the regex or addressing regressions the pure log designs carry.

And the scan-first arithmetic keeps the whole question honest. zstd level 1 decompresses at ~1.3–1.4 GB/s single-core, and zstd decompression speed is roughly independent of the compression level used to write the frame benchmark — which is why VictoriaLogs and ClickHouse can compress hard at write time without paying at query time. LZ4 runs ~4–5 GB/s benchmark. At 1.3 GB/s, a fully decompressed 10 GB hot set costs ~7.7 s single-threaded and trivially parallelises. M1 already demonstrated the endpoint of that argument on real data: 5.73 GB of signal, and 50 ms to regex all of it measured.1

Cross-cutting findings

Six things are visible only when the whole field is laid out at once. None of them appears in any single tool's documentation.

1. The resource failure mode is universal, and throttling does not fix it

Beagle 2006–2009. Baloo, continuously, to at least August 2024. Spotlight's mds_stores, for two decades. Windows Search's SearchIndexer.exe contending for CPU and I/O after any large file operation. Every daemon-based content indexer in this survey, on every platform, has the same failure mode: the background extractor eats a core, or grows unbounded RSS, and the user turns it off.

The evidence that this is not solvable with scheduling primitives is Baloo, which has the most complete set in the survey — nice 19, ioprio idle class, SCHED_IDLE, an unconditional battery gate, and the only default cgroup limits — and the worst reputation.78 The mechanical reason is that every one of those primitives is relative-to-contention; on an idle desktop a SCHED_IDLE process gets a full core for free, and MemoryHigh throttles rather than caps. Recoll's own documentation concedes the point and tells administrators to layer CPUQuota= on top.11 Recoll's choom -n 300 is the only mechanism found anywhere in the survey that targets the memory failure mode directly, by making the indexer the preferred OOM victim.

The correct reading is that the design decision that matters is not how to throttle the work but how much work to do at all — and that is where M1 lands hardest. On the target corpus, 96% of the bytes are build output measured. A wrong exclusion rule costs 25× the index. Getting exclusion right is worth more than every codec in this document combined.

4. Term-dictionary regex is sold as content regex

Stated once more as a standalone finding, because it recurs across every layer of the survey and changes what several rows of the master table mean. Dictionary regex compiles the pattern to an automaton and intersects it with the FST or B-tree, producing an OR of exact-term lookups: fast, proportional to vocabulary rather than corpus, and structurally incapable of matching across token boundaries or inside tokens the analyzer transformed. Content regex inspects bytes. Baloo, Lucene, Tantivy, Bleve, OpenGrok and Elasticsearch all advertise the first; several third-party comparison tables record it as the second. A specification for a new tool should name which one it means in the first sentence of the feature.

5. Nobody has unified filename and content in one format

plocate solves filenames with block-coarsened trigrams and knows nothing about content. zoekt and csearch solve content with trigrams and treat filenames as a separate posting list bolted alongside. Everything indexes the namespace with extreme efficiency and added content only in 1.5, as an optional and separate index. Recoll, Tracker and Baloo index content and treat filenames as another term field with no special structure. Every tool in the survey solves exactly one of the two problems. The one architecture that explicitly splits them — ultrasearch's separate fast-filename process and Tantivy content process — is a Windows-only 2025 project, and the split-process shape, not the implementation, is the interesting part.

6. Everyone converged on the same two engine families, and no LSM

Xapian Glass is a copy-on-write B+-tree. Lucene is segment-and-merge. Baloo is LMDB, another copy-on-write B+-tree. Windows Search is ESE, a B-tree ISAM. SQLite FTS5 is the closest thing to an LSM here, and it is an LSM implemented inside SQLite's own B-tree machinery. Every long-lived desktop search tool in this survey is Xapian-family or Lucene-family, and nothing uses a genuine LSM tree. The one structurally different design — livegrep's suffix array — is the one that does not scale past a bounded corpus. That is a strong prior, and it is worth stating plainly rather than implying: the design space here has been explored, and it converged.

What the survey says to a builder

The intelligence is mostly about what not to spend effort on. Density and codec choice are the field's most-discussed axis and, at the measured corpus size, its least consequential: exclusion policy dominates, and 50 ms of parallel scanning already beats any index that could be built. The axis the field has genuinely failed on, repeatedly and across every platform, is bounding background resource use deterministically — and the survey's own evidence says the standard primitives cannot do it. The capability the field has genuinely failed to ship is indexed content regex in a maintained, embeddable, open form. The structure nobody has built is one on-disk format serving filenames and content coherently. Those three, not posting-list compression, are where the open ground is.
