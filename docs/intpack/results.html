What this run says

ip-pfor128: Lucene's exceptions in intpack's block

ip-bp128 became ip-pfor128: the same 128-block container, but the width is set by the 8th-largest gap and up to seven outliers are patched in as [index][high byte] pairs — Lucene's PForUtil rule. Real posting gaps are heavy-tailed (a typical words.docs block: 127 gaps under 512 and one of 86 045), so plain bitpacking paid 17 bits × 128 for 9-bit data. Result on words.docs: 9.33 bits/int vs lucene-pfor 9.41 and the old 10.14; trigrams 5.57 vs 5.72. With the skip table, ip-pfor128-skip is 9.69 vs lucene-docs 10.36 (trigrams 6.05 vs 6.42). Blocks without exceptions are still byte-identical to the crate rows and take the same fused SIMD path; patched blocks decode in three passes, which is the hot-decode cost (2081 → 1757 Mi/s, still 1.7× Lucene). Encode 348 → 175 (Lucene's port: 144).

The skip cursor now matches Lucene's intersection

A profile of the 1:1000 leapfrog (stopword-length long lists) put 45 of 115 ns/e in a scalar in-block scan, 36 in decode and 19 in the skip-table search — and 45 % of the blocks the cursor landed in were width-0 runs of consecutive ids that were unpacked and scanned anyway. Three cursor-only changes, no format change: answer a run block from its base, scan a block four lanes at a time, and gallop the skip table from the current block instead of re-bisecting it. ∩ 1:1000 on words.docs: 115 → 53.5 ns/e against lucene-docs 53.9 (plain bp128-skip 82); 1:100 45.6 vs 43.4; trigrams 62.5 vs 56.2. Fresh seek 239 vs 508 ns. A fourth change — a same-block fast path that skips the table read and the block dispatch when the target is at most the loaded block's last id — took ∩ 1:1 from 6.6 to 5.3 (Lucene 5.6) and nothing else. A detour worth recording: one build measured 60–62 on words across eight runs while every other build and a byte-identical cp of that same file measured 53–55 — 6× the L1I misses, all L2 fills, tied to the inode's physical pages, not to code layout. The published rows are one binary on a fresh inode.

Unsorted blocks: a frame minimum

Lucene stores an all-equal block as a token plus one vint, which is why it led on term frequencies (4.54 vs 4.60). intpack's unsorted blocks now subtract the block minimum and store it as a varint after the token — the general form of the same shortcut, one byte per block when the minimum is 0. words.freqs 4.33 vs 4.54, words.posdeltas 10.16 vs 10.23; sorted blocks are untouched and byte-identical. Unsorted encode paid 10% (210 Mi/s, Lucene 188); decode is flat because the add rides inside the unpack kernel. A one-pass kernel for sorted blocks with exceptions was also tried and reverted: it moved intersection by nothing, so the remaining trigrams gap is not in decode.

A bitset block kind for dense stretches

Lucene stores a 128-doc block whose span is under 2048 as a bitset and answers advance on it with word operations. intpack's sorted lists now do the same, on the second escape token: [0xfe][first gap][len][len bytes], chosen per block only when strictly smaller than the patched block, so it is a density gain, not a trade — words.docs 9.61 vs 10.36 (was 9.69), trigrams 5.85 vs 6.42 (was 6.05); plain ip-pfor128 9.25 / 5.37. 15 k of words' 95 k full blocks and 44 k of trigrams' 139 k are bitsets. Intersection where the long list is dense: words ∩ 1:1000 41.3 vs 55.3 ns/e (was 53.5), trigrams 48.6 vs 58.9 (was 62.5); 1:100 41.3 vs 45.2 and 28.6 vs 30.5; plain ip-pfor128 1:1000 344 → 228. Hot decode is unchanged to slightly up (1875 / 1911 Mi/s vs Lucene's 1054 / 994: a table-driven byte-to-positions decode is no slower than unpacking the blocks it replaces). The first cut regressed trigrams ∩ 1:10 by 1.8 ns/e, confirmed on three fresh inodes; a perf phase split found it was not the bitset work but a call: the new arm pushed the cursor's enter past LLVM's inlining threshold, so every next() and fast-path miss paid a six-register call whose body was mostly the early return. Keeping the check inline and the dispatch out of line, plus a one-word successor walk for next() on a bitset, put it back (+22 lines). +667 lines in intpack over the two slices, most of it tests.

The in-block scan, sixteen lanes wide

With everything else at or ahead, ∩ 1:10 was still 1.7 ns/e behind on both corpora, and two cursor slices had failed to move it. A perf split of both codecs at that one phase (cycles with dwarf stacks, harness frame separated) put the two leaf tables side by side for the first time: decode 3.4 vs 6.8 ns/e in intpack's favour, the in-block scan 9.4 vs 4.6 against it — a 1:10 hop is ~10 ids, so a scan pays per compare-and-branch round, not per lane, and the 4-lane SSE2 loop ran twice as many rounds as Lucene's scalar one. scan_geq now has a sixteen-lane arm under target_feature = "avx512f" (cmpge_epu32_mask straight to a bitmask; SSE2 stays the arm elsewhere), plus a 12-line empty-asm! pin that stops LLVM hoisting the plain cursor's needle broadcast out of its loop and spilling it across every block entry. words ∩ 1:10 23.9 vs 26.1 (was 28.0), trigrams 17.7 vs 17.1 (was 19.2; Lucene's own runs span 16.7–17.5); trigrams ∩ 1:1 9.3 → 6.9 vs 11.8; fresh seek −6% on both. +64 lines, cursor only.

Where Lucene still leads

On the corpora, nowhere outside run noise: trigrams ∩ 1:10 is 17.7 vs 17.1 (−3%, inside the band both codecs show run to run) and the remaining ip-only cost there is the harness's Adapt tail-call thunk, not the cursor. On synthetics: periodic-s100-j0 ∩ 1:1 13.0 vs 9.7 and uniform-d0.5 20.9 vs 18.2 — every block a bitset or a fixed stride, where Lucene's element-by-element next is leaner than ours; zipf-dict-e1-b0 ∩ 1:10 32.1 vs 24.4 (1:100 and 1:1000 are 30% ahead). ip-fastpfor remains the density reference on long clustered lists (1.20 bits/int on clustered-d0.01-b64 vs ip-pfor128's 5.23; its page format allows unlimited exceptions) but pays a per-list header that costs it 11.97 on words.docs.

Every other ip-* codec matches or beats the crate it mirrors

ip-vbyte and ip-fastpfor emit the same bytes as (or fewer than) their crates and decode within noise of them — ip-vbyte hot decode 1171 vs 701 Mi/s on words.docs. ip-fastpfor's own page layout is denser than the port and both C++ builds: 11.97 bits/int vs fastpfor128 12.83 and cpp-optpfor 12.59 on words.docs, at 3× the port's encode rate.

ip-ef makes Elias-Fano usable on short lists

sucds spends 60 bits/int of select index on words.docs (1.4 M lists, 11.5 ints each); ip-ef omits samples when the highs fit in 16 words and carries a two-level 1024/64 index only on long lists: 11.83 vs 113.57 bits/int on words.docs, 6.65 vs 9.29 on trigrams.docs, while get (36 vs 44 ns), fresh seek (167 vs 171) and 1:1000 intersect (54 vs 72 ns/e) all lead. On words.docs it is now the second-densest seekable codec after bp128-skip with a faster seek.

ip-pef: identical partitions, 1.5–3.7× faster seek

Byte-identical to the reference pef on every dataset (15.47 / 5.30 / 5.52 on words / trigrams / uniform-d0.1) with an upper-level select index the reference lacks: fresh seek 282 vs 440 ns (words), 263 vs 475 (trigrams), 282 vs 1046 (uniform-d0.1); 1:1000 intersect 162 vs 206 and 159 vs 229 ns/e; decode 195 vs 123 Mi/s. pef remains the density winner on clustered data (2.70 on clustered-d0.01-b512; 5.30 on trigrams at the 5.32 bound).

Where intpack is still behind

ip-streamvbyte decodes at 1590 vs 2062 Mi/s (−23%) on words.docs and 1588 vs 2088 on trigrams; the streamvbyte64 crate unrolls eight 4-int groups per tag word. Not pursued — streamvbyte is the least dense codec here (12.90 vs bp128's 10.14 on words.docs) and not a candidate under density-over-speed. Roaring keeps the fastest random access on words.docs (get 16 ns, 1:1000 at 23 ns/e) but at 24.94 bits/int.
