One place: the in-block scan. A 1:10 hop is ~10 ids, so the scan pays per compare-and-branch round; the 4-lane SSE2 loop ran twice the rounds of Lucene's scalar scan and the cheaper decode had been hiding it. Fix (intpack 8bd715d + d0cf41b, +64 lines, cursor only): a sixteen-lane scan_geq under target_feature = "avx512f", SSE2 kept as the other arm; and a 12-line empty-asm! pin(target) because LLVM then hoisted the plain cursor's needle broadcast out of its loop and spilled the zmm across every enter_block (plain ∩ 1:1 8.5 → 6.8). Dave (2026-09-22): keep the asm!, but document it so a Rust upgrade triggers a recheck; and the AVX-512-only arm "needs to be well documented". Done as intpack 6423815: src/toolchain.rs is a ledger of every compiler-steering item — the asm! pin and the five inlining attributes in the cursors — each with the bench row and the number that justified it; its test compares the compiling rustc (recorded by a 6-line build.rs) against the version they were measured with (1.95.0 / LLVM 21.1.8) and fails with the recheck recipe when the toolchain moves. The SIMD scan arms are no longer on that list: both now compile for tests on any x86_64 and scan_agrees_on_random_blocks runs every arm the host can execute under runtime detection, so a baseline cargo test covers the sixteen-lane arm with no flags. Gates green at baseline and under target-cpu=native; 101 tests.

Q4 · The ∩ 1:10 residue: words −11%, trigrams −24%. Stop, or one more slice?

The question: is the ∩ 1:10 gap worth another cursor slice, given that the bitset kind halved it on words and widened it on trigrams?

What the trigrams regression says: a 1:10 walk lands in most blocks of the long list. On a packed block the cost is one unpack plus SSE scan set-up for ~13 hits (≈6 ns/e amortised plus set-up); on a bitset block each landing walks words from the previous hit with a popcount per word (next_set(from, i)), and a 32-word body with hits ~10 bits apart means ~2 words per landing — cheap, but the entry into a bitset from a table search starts at word 0 and pops every word up to the target, and trigrams has 44 k bitset blocks against words' 15 k. That is a guess from the shape of the code; it has not been profiled.

The subagent's perf phase split (same session, pre- and post-bitset binaries alternating, new bench --only <phase>) found the guess above was wrong in an instructive way: the bitset work itself got cheaper on the packed side (−2.2 ns/e) and the cost was call overhead — the Loaded::Bits arm pushed SortedCursor::enter past LLVM's inlining threshold, so every next() and fast-path miss paid a call whose body was 55% early-return epilogue (+0.9), and next() on a bitset called the general next_set out of line (+1.1). Fix: check inline, dispatch in #[inline(never)] load; a one-word successor for next(). intpack 84481df, +22 lines, cursor only. Published run (one binary, fresh inode): trigrams ∩ 1:10 19.2 vs 17.4 (−10%, back to pre-bitset), words 28.0 vs 26.3 (−6%), uniform-d0.5 ∩ 1:1 20.7 vs 17.8; nothing else moved. The remaining 1:10 residue turned out to be one place after all — see Q5.

Q3 · The ∩ 1:10 gap: accept it, or add a bitset block?

Where the goal stands (after the lean-cursor slice): density ahead on every dataset (words.docs 9.33 vs 9.41, freqs 4.33 vs 4.54, posdeltas 10.16 vs 10.23, trigrams 5.57 vs 5.72); hot decode 1.7×, fresh seek 2.1×, encode 1.1× Lucene; ∩ 1:1 ahead (5.3 vs 5.6 words, 9.1 vs 11.4 trigrams); ∩ 1:1000 words tie (53.5 vs 53.9). Behind at ∩ 1:10: words 33.3 vs 25.9 (−29%), trigrams 19.2 vs 16.8 (−14%), and by 5–11% at 1:100 and trigrams 1:1000 (62.5 vs 56.2; Lucene's 1:1000 swings ±4 run to run).

What the 1:10 profile says (.ai/DONE-ix10.md): the pairs are median 90 × 900 ints in a 334 k universe — not stopword lists. The whole 7.4 ns/e gap is inside our next_geq body (20.8 vs 14.0 ns/e); block decode is identical for both (≈6 ns/e); the leapfrog frame is the same. Inside next_geq: prologue/skip-search 4.7, enter 2.0, scan setup 4.8, scan loop 2.9, hit commit 6.3. Lucene spends its 14 in a scalar inc/cmp scan (~5) and not much else. Only half the landings are blocks Lucene stores as bitsets, and its next_set_bit path is invisible in the samples.

Q2 · Trigrams ∩ 1:1000 was 4 ns/e behind — chase it?

Options were: accept; find the "alignment-sensitive loop"; bitset block. Two investigations resolved it without a decision: the one-pass patched-decode kernel moved intersection by nothing (parked on wt/pfor-fused), and the 54.8 / 62.0 build-to-build spread turned out to be one binary's inode (6× L1I misses, all L2 fills, gone on cp), not code. On a fresh inode trigrams is 63.1 vs 61.5 (−3%) and words is ahead.

Superseded by Q3: the 1:10 gap is larger and structural; 1:1000 is within noise.

Q1 · Close the 0.06 bits/int on words.freqs?

Options were: frame-of-reference minimum on unsorted blocks (recommended); Lucene's all-equal shortcut; leave it. Taken on the recommendation since the goal stood and the slice was cheap and reversible.

Option 1 landed (intpack 886584e + 54a229c): freqs 4.33 vs 4.54, posdeltas 10.16 vs 10.23; unsorted encode −10%, decode flat (the add rides inside the unpack kernel), sorted path byte-identical. An all-equal block is now [token 0][vbyte min], which is Lucene's shortcut for free.
